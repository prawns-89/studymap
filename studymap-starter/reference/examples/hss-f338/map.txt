%A | Comparative | Part 1 | What "comparative" means
@complit | Comparative literature: two approaches | Comparative lit | 1
- The study of literatures in different languages, compared with one another.
- Approach 1: pick a **genre**. Example: the European novel of the 18th century, comparing French, German, Italian and English novels side by side.
- Approach 2: pick a **theme or idea**. Example: the idea of the nation: how languages and literatures express what a nation is, what belonging means, what national identity means.
- Memory hook: **genre across languages, or idea across languages**. Past papers phrase it as "genre and theme" (distractors: plot and setting; point of view and conflict; language and characters). {2023,2024}
> requirements | what comparison needs
> method_discipline | method and discipline
> valmiki_vs_kamban | the method in action: one story, two languages
@requirements | What comparison needs, and how far it reaches | Scope of comparison | 2
- You need to know **at least two languages** and use them to read and compare literary texts (past-paper answer; distractors one, three, four). {2023,2024}
- Comparison works **across time**: classical and medieval texts alongside modern ones, e.g. a Sanskrit text alongside a Hindi text.
- It isn't limited to literature vs literature: you can compare literature with **other art forms, e.g. film**.
- So a pair that stays inside one language, one period and one form does **not** qualify: modern Bengali novel vs modern Bengali short story (past-paper answer). Hindi film vs Hindi short story qualifies (art forms); classical Sanskrit drama vs contemporary Gujarati drama qualifies (time and language); Spanish novel vs German novel qualifies (languages). {2023,2024}

> modernity | a product of modernity
@memory_forgetting | Memory and forgetting | Memory & forgetting | 1
- A nation is built through **shared memory and selective forgetting**: past triumphs and accomplishments are kept; episodes of violence, brutality and humiliation are forgotten.
- Nation-building wants **unity and homogeneity**, usually resting on a common language, culture, religion, territory and history. "The essence of a nation is that all individuals have many things in common."
- Race, religion, language and geography are vital to how a nation is imagined. History gives the nation a body (**"corporeality"**). The nation is treated as sacred, immune to change and inflexible. It is also the will of the people; secession is a change in territorial span.
- The lecture then asks: was nationalism the product of a sense of community that already existed?
- So **heterogeneity** among the population is contrary to the general assumptions about a nation (past-paper answer; homogeneity, remembering triumphs and forgetting violence all fit). {2023,2024}
- The notes add that the memory-and-forgetting idea comes from **Ernest Renan**'s lecture "What is a Nation?" {notes+}
> gellner | theories of nationalism
> smith | "shared myths and historical memories"
@gellner | Ernest Gellner | Gellner | 1
- Nationalism is connected to, and decided by, the **needs of industrial society**. Modernity is the foundation of nations and nationalisms.
- Industrial society shakes up old identity markers and class differences. **Education** produces a degree of homogenisation: everyone learns the same standard culture.
- But moulding people into one homogeneous whole doesn't always work: "high" culture can coexist with peasant and artisan cultures.
- In one line: nationalism is a product of industrial modernity, spread through education.
@smith | Anthony D. Smith | A. D. Smith | 1
- The nation: "Populations possessing a historic similarity, **shared myths and historical memories**, a mass, **public culture**, a **single economy** and **common rights and duties** for all members, which are legitimized by the principles of nationalism."
@anderson | Benedict Anderson | Anderson | 1
- The nation is an **"imagined community"**: you will never meet most of your fellow nationals, yet you feel bound to them.
- It depends on **print-capitalism** (newspapers and novels) and is propped up by three pieces of modern apparatus: the **census, the map and the museum**.

@fundamental_unity | The fundamental unity of India | Fundamental unity | 2
- Despite huge differences, there was and is enough in common in literary and intellectual traditions to study this part of the world as a unit. Cultural continuity between many texts supports this.
- You needn't fully agree that the commonality was invented by Western Orientalists. All commonality is invented, but this invention came **centuries before the British**.
- The evidence: ancient texts and the epics **list the regions** of what is now India and describe their peoples. More distant known regions, such as **Greece**, are not included.
> boundaries | yet boundaries are fluid
@boundaries | Geographical boundaries | Boundaries | 2

%D | One or many | Part 4 | Indian Literature or Literatures?
@concept_il | The concept of Indian Literature | The concept | 2
- Hard to define: it isn't one literature written in one language.
- Defining a literature is easier when the nation is a clearly definable cultural entity, because culture and literature are closely linked. India isn't an easy cultural entity to define, so **both the singular and the plural** are used.
@nilavilakku | Nilavilakku: many wicks, one light | Nilavilakku | 1
- "Many tongues, one voice." There is no common Indian language, but there is a common Indian literature, depending on how you look at it.
- The analogy is the traditional Indian **lamp with many wicks**, the Nilavilakku, from **K. M. George**. Past papers call it "**base lamps**" (distractors: temple bells, anklets, chandelier). {2023,2024}
- "The lamp is the same, the oil is the same, but it is the wicks that determine the brightness of light. The lights from various wicks merge imperceptibly and produce a brightness which is the totality of many lights. Just as many wicks produce one light, India's many languages produce one literature."
- Lamp = India. Oil = the shared sources. Wicks = the languages. One light = Indian Literature.
- This map is drawn as that lamp: each cluster is a wick around the light at the centre.
@unity_diversity | Unity in diversity | Unity in diversity | 2
- Each language community expresses its own experience distinctly, but India's geopolitical and cultural territories have always been **porous**. Cultures give and receive; there is a spiritual oneness.
- Points of convergence are **thematic, generic and ideological**, and rarely confined to one language or region. Literature explores beyond narrow confines.
@civilisation | Indian civilisation vs Macaulay | Macaulay | 1
- The concept of "**Bharatvarsha**" took on new significance and meaning in **colonial** times (past-paper answer; distractors Buddhist revival, medieval, ancient). {2023,2024}
- **T. B. Macaulay**: "a single shelf of a good European library was worth the whole native literature of India" (past-paper distractors: James Mill, Robert Clive, Friedrich Max Müller). {2023,2024}
- The "civilizing mission" and "the white man's burden". Civilisation: "an advanced state of human society, in which a high level of culture, science, industry, and government has been reached."
- So asserting the glory and antiquity of Indian civilisation became part of the nationalist project.

@commonality | Commonality in Indian literature | Commonality | 1
- Most Indian writers, **from Kabir to A. K. Ramanujan**, are bilingual if not multilingual.

@literary_past | India's literary past | Literary past | 3
- A literary history should account for the whole heritage, however scattered or loose.
- Literature in India is as old as its sculpture, painting or music, but hasn't received the historical attention those arts have.
- There is a common literary past; Indian Literature means literatures in Indian languages, classical and modern, and has to be inclusive.
- The paradox: **homogenisation is dangerous, but pure heterogeneity erases what cultures share**. That tension is the whole course in one sentence.
> sahitya_akademi | after Independence
@sahitya_akademi | Sahitya Akademi | Sahitya Akademi | 1
- Formally inaugurated by the Government of India on **12 March 1954**.
- The Government Resolution: a national organisation to develop Indian letters, set high literary standards, foster and co-ordinate literary activity in all Indian languages, and through them **promote the cultural unity of the country**. Past-paper answer to "mandated to... forging national cultural unity" (distractors: National Translation Mission, Sahitya Samvad, Central Institute of Indian Languages). {2023,2024}
- India's **National Academy of Letters**, the central institution for literary dialogue, publication and promotion.
- The only institution doing literary work in **24 Indian languages, including English**.
- Works through seminars, lectures, symposia, discussions, readings, performances, translation workshops, journals, monographs, anthologies, encyclopaedias, dictionaries, bibliographies, who's-who of writers and literary histories.
- Over **6,000 books**, at a pace of **one book every 19 hours**.
- **24 awards** a year for literary works and an equal number for literary **translations**, each after a year-long scrutiny.
- **Bhasha Samman**: for significant contributions to languages the Akademi doesn't formally recognise, and to classical and medieval literature.
@perception_reality | Perception vs reality | Perception vs reality | 1
- Indian Literature is often confused with ancient works in **Sanskrit**. A modern, forward-looking concept of Indian Literature has not been formulated.
- The thesis "India has one literature that is written in many languages" has **never been properly explored and validated**.
- **Tamil is the only language with a continuous literary tradition.** Its early grammar, the **Tolkappiyam**, recognises exactly one other language as its equal and competitor: **Sanskrit**.
- So what is classical Indian literature: Tamil? Sanskrit? Tamil and Sanskrit? The slide leaves the question mark.
- The epics exist in **folk and written** traditions. Folk and popular traditions often oppose each other and the literary. There is **symbiosis between Tamil and Sanskrit**.
